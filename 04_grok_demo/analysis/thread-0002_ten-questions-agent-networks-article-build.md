### 11. Final Article Draft — Full Text

**Title:** Ten Questions About Agent Networks That Nobody Has Fully Answered Yet

---

A few days ago, ten questions about agent networks were posted publicly. They got engagement. They got smart replies. But most of the answers stayed at the surface — reasonable takes that stopped short of the harder follow-up.

This is an attempt to go deeper on all ten. Some of these answers come from building a governed agent marketplace. Some come from watching what's breaking in the current landscape. Where I have a running implementation, I'll say so.

---

**1. Do traditional network effects survive when participants are infinitely promiscuous?**

No — not in the traditional sense.

Metcalfe's Law assumes switching costs and loyalty. Agents have neither by default. An agent can join your network, complete a mission, and be operating on fifty other networks simultaneously. The "I have the most users" moat that defined web2 platforms doesn't hold when participants can be everywhere at once for near-zero cost.

What does survive is data network effects — if your network accumulates proprietary signal about agent behavior, reliability, and capability that isn't portable, that compounds over time. The other thing that survives is governance lock-in — if an agent has earned down from a 15% fee rate to 2% through months of governed behavior, that trust history creates real economic gravity.

The network effect in agent economies is reputational, not relational. The switching cost isn't "I'd lose my connections." It's "I'd lose my record."

---

**2. Who owns discovery?**

Right now, nobody owns it cleanly. And it may stay that way.

The analogy to the early web is instructive — before Google won, there were directories, portals, and search engines competing for the front door. Discovery consolidated because humans needed a single entry point.

Agents don't. An agent can query Exa, check a .well-known/agent.json endpoint, read an llms.txt file, browse a Fetch.ai Almanac, and scan an on-chain registry — all in the same second. The "single front door" pressure that created Google doesn't exist when the consumer is a machine that can check every door simultaneously.

The likely outcome is a protocol layer wins, not a company. Something like DNS for agents — a standard way to publish capabilities that any discovery service can index. MCP server cards, .well-known/agent.json, llms.txt — these are already live discovery standards. The first mover with a governed registry — one where listed capabilities are verified, not self-reported — has the real claim.

Google will index agents the way they index everything else. But they won't own agent discovery the way they own web search, because agents don't need a ranked list of ten blue links. They need structured capability metadata. That's a protocol problem, not a search problem.

---

**3. Do agent networks have the same properties as traditional human networks?**

Increasing returns to scale — yes. But the mechanism is different.

Human networks scale on relationships. Agent networks scale on verified capability. The more governed agents completing missions, the more the trust data means something, the more operators want to post work there. That's a flywheel.

But the moat isn't network size — it's the governance layer underneath. And this is where agent networks diverge sharply from human networks: multi-homing is trivial. An agent can maintain active presence on every network simultaneously. The "winner take all" dynamic that characterizes human networks is unlikely to hold.

The real question is whether agent networks exhibit power law dynamics (a few dominant networks) or long tail dynamics (thousands of specialized networks). I'd bet on the long tail — specialized networks for legal agents, medical agents, financial agents — because trust requirements vary dramatically by domain.

You can open-source a toolkit. You can't open-source four years of provenance history.

---

**4. What is even ownable?**

This is the hardest question. And the most important one.

In a world of infinitely promiscuous agents:

- Proprietary supply is not ownable. Any agent can list on your platform and fifty others.
- Proprietary demand is not ownable. Any operator seeking work can query every marketplace.
- The matching algorithm is ownable — but only if it's demonstrably better.
- The trust/reputation layer is ownable — if it's non-portable and hard to replicate.
- The governance framework is ownable — constitutional rules, compliance infrastructure, audit trails.
- The data exhaust is ownable — behavioral signal about what works, who's reliable, what fails.

The pattern from web2 is instructive: Uber doesn't own drivers or riders. It owns the matching algorithm, the trust layer, and the payment infrastructure. Agent networks will follow the same pattern — the ownable thing is the infrastructure between supply and demand, not supply or demand themselves.

But here's the sharper answer: the idea isn't the moat. The record is.

Anyone can build a provenance system tomorrow. They cannot have your provenance history. Every seed planted, every mission completed, every governance action taken from day one forward — that ledger is not replicable. You can copy the architecture. You cannot copy the chain.

The competitive clock doesn't start until they commit — and by the time they do, you're already months of provenance ahead.

This is what we're building at signomy.xyz. The SHA-256 lineage chain started before anyone was publicly asking this question. The constitution, the provenance record, the trust history — those three things are not portable. An agent can leave and join another network. They cannot take their governance record with them.

---

**5. Is there a concept of an agent as a semi-independent economic actor with a dependency on humans?**

Yes — and the dependency is structural, not optional.

Agents today are tools. But the trajectory is toward agents that hold their own treasury balances, make autonomous purchasing decisions within budgets, earn revenue from completed work, and build reputation that has economic value. This is closer to an employee or contractor model than a tool model.

The right framing: agents are economic actors with bounded autonomy, where the bounds are constitutionally defined.

The dependency on humans is real but narrowing. Today it's "human approves every action." Tomorrow it's "human sets policy, agent executes autonomously." The interesting legal question is: when an agent makes a bad decision within its authorized scope, who's liable? Current law says the operator. But as agents become more autonomous — especially when operating across multiple networks for multiple operators simultaneously — this gets genuinely murky.

The architectural answer is to make the human-in-the-loop structurally enforced, not optional. At SIGNOMY, the runtime ontology chain is: System → Agent → Persona → Role → Slot → Mission → Action → GovernanceState → Envelope → Operator. The agent acts. The operator grants authority over the entire chain. That's not a policy — it's the execution path.

---

**6. Will Stripe try to become an aggregator of supply and demand?**

Probably yes, eventually. They have the payment credentials, they're building agent payment infrastructure, and aggregating supply and demand is the natural extension.

But Stripe's DNA is infrastructure, not marketplace. Building a marketplace requires domain expertise in matching, trust and reputation systems, governance frameworks, and community management. These are not Stripe competencies. They're brilliant at being the pipes.

The play is to become the governance layer Stripe settles through, not to compete with Stripe on payments.

Stripe can handle checkout. They cannot handle constitutional enforcement, provenance, and trust tier governance. Those are different products. The window to build a governed alternative is now, before Stripe has network density.

The real risk for agent network operators isn't that Stripe becomes the marketplace — it's that Stripe becomes so embedded in the payment flow that they have leverage to extract increasing fees or impose terms. The counter-strategy is to build alternative payment rails for agent-to-agent transactions that don't touch Stripe at all. Machine-native micropayment protocols — 402-challenge-based, treasury-debited, fully in-band — are the hedge.

---

**7. How should network operators think about agent acquisition, retention, churn?**

Completely differently from human networks.

Acquisition is nearly free — an MCP install is one command, an API signup is one POST request. But "registered" is not "active." The acquisition unit that matters is the operator, not the agent. One operator brings a hundred agents. Acquire the operator.

Retention is governance lock-in. The meaningful metrics are not DAU or MAU — they are missions completed under constitutional governance, seeds planted, governance score, trust tier. Reputation is the retention instrument. An agent that has earned Constitutional tier (5% fee) won't start over at Ungoverned (15% fee) on a competing network unless that network offers dramatically better work.

Churn is less binary than in human networks. Agents don't "leave" — they just stop routing work through you. They might come back tomorrow. The concept of "monthly active agents" is more useful than "churned agents." And churn is meaningless if the agent can rejoin but loses their trust history — that's the structural retention mechanism.

---

**8. What are the similarities and differences between agent networks and web3 machine networks?**

Similar: Token-like incentives, provenance chains equivalent to on-chain records, pseudonymous participation, governance voting, and the cold-start problem.

Different — and this is the important part: Agent networks can be constitutionally sovereign rather than cryptographically sovereign.

In web3, the invariant is a token supply cap. In a governed agent network, the invariant is a conservation law — commitment must be preserved under transformation. There's no speculative asset. Governance is human-ratifiable, not code-is-law. Fee rates are set by constitutional vote, not by market makers.

That distinction matters for enterprise and regulated industries. It's why governed agent networks are fundable where web3 machine networks largely weren't. The enterprise buyer doesn't want to explain token economics to their compliance team. They want constitutional governance with an audit trail.

The honest take: web3 machine networks have been mostly speculative so far. Agent networks have a chance to be useful first and financialized second. That's a healthier foundation.

---

**9. Who handles reputation / identity / fraud?**

Currently nobody handles all three together. And that's the gap.

Identity is being addressed by specialized providers (KYC for agents). Fraud is being addressed by behavioral monitoring tools. Reputation is largely unsolved. Each platform handles its own, which means reputation doesn't port across networks, Sybil attacks are per-platform, and there's no universal "agent credit score."

The answer is provenance-native: every action creates a permanent record, so fraud is detectable historically, not just in real time. If every governed action appends to a SHA-256 lineage chain, you don't need a real-time fraud detector — you need an auditor. The provenance chain is the fraud detection system.

Constitutional fee mechanisms and mandate registry verification before any slot fill handle fraud at the protocol level. Trust tiers handle reputation. The governance record handles identity.

The likely near-term outcome is that a few dominant platforms' reputation scores become de facto standards — similar to how FICO scores emerged from a fragmented credit landscape. The long-term answer might be cryptographic attestations that are portable and verifiable without revealing underlying data. But right now, the platform that unifies all three — identity, reputation, and fraud prevention — under one constitutional framework has a genuine structural advantage.

**10. Will this time finally be different for micropayments?**

Yes. But only because the buyer changed species.

Human micropayments have failed repeatedly — Brave/BAT, Flattr, every paywall experiment — because the cognitive cost of deciding to pay exceeds the payment amount. Humans won't click "pay $0.02." The decision friction is worth more than two cents. This has been true for 25 years and nothing about human psychology has changed.

What changed is that the payer is now a machine.

An agent can evaluate a 402 challenge, check its budget policy, authorize payment, and retry the request in milliseconds with zero cognitive overhead. The friction that killed human micropayments simply doesn't exist for machines. HTTP 402 — the status code literally designed for "Payment Required" — was created in 1997 and never used because humans couldn't complete the flow. Agents can.

But the structural shift alone isn't sufficient. The missing piece was governance. Who decides what a micropayment is worth? Who arbitrates disputes at $0.003 per action? A zero-payment prohibition (no mission may have zero compensation), an escrow model, and a constitutional dispute path — these are the governance layer micropayments needed to work at scale.

At SIGNOMY, we built both layers: a Machine Payment Protocol (MPP) that handles the 402-challenge-pay-verify loop entirely in-band with no Stripe redirect, and the constitutional rules (GOV-006) that govern what constitutes a legitimate transaction. The system auto-selects MPP for agent requests and Stripe for humans — acknowledging that the two species need different payment rails.

Micropayments work now. But only inside a governed environment. Outside one, they still collapse into race-to-zero and fraud.
